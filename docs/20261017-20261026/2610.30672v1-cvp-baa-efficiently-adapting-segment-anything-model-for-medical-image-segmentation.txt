Abstract
Various task-specific medical segmentation models have demonstrated strong perfor-
mance, their heavy reliance on extensive annotations and highly specialized architectures
limits their versatility, scalability, and clinical applicability. Recently, adapting vision
foundation models, particularly the segment anything model (SAM), has emerged as
a promising alternative. However, prevalent parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT)
strategies, such as prompt tuning and adapter tuning, often lack an explicit mechanism
to embed local structural priors into the long-range dependency modeling of vision
transformers (ViT), rendering them insufficiently sensitive to fine-grained details. To
address these limitations, we introduce CVP-BAA, a novel hybrid PEFT method that
improves SAM’s sensitivity to domain-specific textures and local details, leading to
superior boundary delineation. The proposed CVP-BAA adopts two key ideas. First, it
introduces a convolutional visual prompting (CVP) strategy that leverages convolutional
features as structural priors, prepending them to the key and value projections within
the multi-head self-attention layers of strategically selected ViT blocks. Through a
dedicated prompt generation-and-injection module, this mechanism effectively bridges
local spatial details with global context semantics. Second, a boundary-aware adapter
(BAA) is designed as a parallel branch to the feed-forward network within the same se-
lected blocks. By explicitly computing and amplifying high-frequency components, the

1. Introduction
In recent years, deep learning has substantially advanced medical image segmenta-
tion [1]. Convolutional models excel at capturing local spatial details, while transformer
architectures are highly effective at modeling long-range dependencies [2, 3]. Hybrid
frameworks that integrate their respective strengths have further boosted segmentation
performance [4, 5, 6]. Nevertheless, most existing approaches are task-specific and
rely heavily on extensive, manually curated annotations, limiting their adaptability,
flexibility, and scalability in clinical practice.
To address the issue, adapting vision foundation models, particularly the segment
anything model (SAM) [7], has emerged as a promising paradigm for medical image
segmentation. Pretrained on massive natural image corpora, SAM has demonstrated
remarkable generalization and promptable segmentation capabilities [8]. However,
directly applying SAM to medical imaging often yields suboptimal performance [9, 10].
This gap primarily stems from a substantial domain shift between natural and medical
images. Medical images frequently exhibit modality-specific characteristics, such as
low tissue contrast, pervasive noise, and imaging artifacts, that are underrepresented
in natural image pretraining distributions. Furthermore, SAM’s underlying vision
transformer (ViT) encoder, while highly effective at modeling global dependencies,
inherently lacks explicit mechanisms to preserve local spatial structures and fine-grained
boundary details [7, 11], yet clinical segmentation demands precise delineation of subtle
anatomical structures and irregular lesion boundaries. As a result, SAM often struggles
to balance global semantics with fine-grained structural information in medical imaging
2


scenarios. Therefore, developing locality-aware adaptation strategies is essential to
efficiently transfer SAM for precise medical image segmentation.
Recent studies have explored various fine-tuning strategies to better adapt SAM
for medical image segmentation [9, 12, 8]. A straightforward approach is full fine-
tuning — updating all parameters of the SAM model. However, this is often impractical
or infeasible for large-scale ViT architectures due to their high computational cost,
which becomes increasingly prohibitive as model size grows. Moreover, full fine-
tuning on limited medical datasets carries significant risks of catastrophic forgetting
and overfitting [13, 14]. To address these constraints under data-scarce conditions,
MedSAM [12] employs a partial component fine-tuning strategy [15] by freezing the
encoder and updating only the mask decoder. This approach demonstrates improved
segmentation performance over the original SAM on medical images. Another line
of work leverages parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) techniques to adapt SAM
with minimal trainable parameters. These include low-rank adaptation (LoRA) [16],
visual prompt tuning (VPT) [17], and adapter modules [18]. Representative PEFT-based
methods for medical segmentation include SAMed [9], which applies LoRA to the ViT
encoder; SAMUS [13], which augments the encoder with bottleneck adapters and a
parallel convolutional neural network (CNN) branch; and Med-SA [8], which employs
prompt-conditioned adapters with space-depth transpose for 3D volumetric adaptation.
Despite these advances, existing adaptation strategies often fail to fully capture the
domain-specific characteristics of medical images, such as low contrast, heterogeneous
textures, and complex anatomical structures [19], and may still yield suboptimal results,
particularly when segmenting targets with intricate or ambiguous boundaries.
As illustrated in Fig. 1, several recent studies have sought to incorporate local infor-
mation, typically extracted via CNNs, into attention mechanisms through cross-attention
modules [13, 20], such local cues are generally not integrated into the self-attention
computation itself. Consequently, the implicit infusion of local information remains
insufficient for achieving precise, fine-grained segmentation of intricate anatomical
structures and lesion boundaries in medical imaging. In addition, many existing adapter-
based methods usually originate from generic adaptation paradigms developed for
computer vision or natural language processing tasks, and thus fail to explicitly ac-

• We propose a novel Convolutional Visual Prompt Tuning (CVP) strategy that

2. Related Work
2.1. Task-Specific Approaches
Medical image segmentation is a cornerstone of computer-aided diagnosis, en-
abling precise anatomical delineation and lesion quantification. Over the past decade,
deep learning has driven substantial progress through several evolving architectural
paradigms. Initially, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) dominated the field by lever-
aging strong inductive biases such as local receptive fields and translation equivariance.
Representative U-shaped architectures like U-Net [2], CE-Net [22], and AAU-Net [23]
effectively preserve spatial details through skip connections and multi-scale feature
5


aggregation. Despite these advancements, the inherent locality of convolutional op-
erations fundamentally restricts their ability to model long-range dependencies, often
leading to suboptimal performance when segmenting targets with large shape variations
or complex lesion structures.
To overcome this issue, transformer-based architectures have been increasingly
adopted. Models such as Swin-Unet [3] and MISSFormer [24] replace convolutional
encoders with hierarchical self-attention mechanisms, enabling global context modeling
across the entire image. Nevertheless, such pure transformers lack inherent spatial
inductive biases, making them prone to over-smoothing fine-grained boundaries and
struggling with low-contrast or small-scale medical targets, especially under limited
annotations. Thus, hybrid CNN-Transformer architectures have emerged to combine
local feature extraction with global semantic modeling. TransUNet [25] pioneers this
direction by feeding CNN-extracted feature maps into a transformer encoder and fusing
high-resolution CNN features via skip connections. TransFuse [4] adopts a parallel
dual-branch design, integrating CNN and transformer pathways through a bidirectional
fusion module to simultaneously capture local details and global semantics. Similarly,
H2Former [26] constructs a hierarchical hybrid block that unifies convolution, multi-
scale channel attention, and token-wise self-attention within a single stage. While these
hybrid designs improve segmentation accuracy, they often rely on complex, manually
engineered fusion strategies and remain heavily dependent on task-specific architectural
tuning.
Furthermore, the quadratic computational complexity of vanilla self-attention has
spurred research into efficient attention mechanisms. Recent works like BRAU-Net++ [5],
MedFormer [27], and MSLAU-Net [28] introduce sparse [29, 30] or linear attention [31]
to scale to higher resolutions. Although these methods significantly reduce compu-
tational burdens, they still require extensive task-specific architectural redesign and
struggle to generalize across diverse imaging modalities without retraining. This moti-
vates a move away from task-specific architectural engineering toward adapting vision
foundation models.

2.2. Segment Anything Model Adaptations
While adapting SAM for domain-specific tasks has garnered more attention, achiev-
ing effective and robust adaptation for medical image segmentation remains a critical
challenge. PEFT has emerged as a promising paradigm, freezing the majority of pre-
trained weights while updating only a minimal, targeted subset. This strategy drastically
reduces computational and memory overhead while preserving the rich, domain-agnostic
representations learned in the foundation model. Existing PEFT-based adaptations for
SAM can be broadly categorized into three representative categories: prompt-based
tuning [17, 32], adapter-based tuning [33, 34], and low-rank adaptation [16].
Prompt-based methods, such as TP-SAM [35] and BT-SAM [36], introduce learn-
able continuous vectors into the input or embedding space to steer SAM toward down-
stream tasks. While highly parameter-efficient and effective at preserving pretrained
knowledge, these prompts primarily operate at the input level. Consequently, they offer
limited capacity to explicitly model local texture patterns, fine-grained anatomical struc-
tures, and ambiguous boundaries that are paramount in medical imaging. Adapter-based
strategies insert lightweight bottleneck modules into transformer layers to bridge the
domain gap. Early approaches adopted the conventional adapter design [18], which, de-
spite its efficiency, lacks domain-specific inductive biases and often struggles to capture
intricate local details. To mitigate this, recent works such as Trans-SAM [37], NAS-
Adapter [38], CC-SAM [39] and SAMUS [13] have integrated convolutional operations
or parallel CNN branches into the adapter architecture, thereby injecting local spatial
priors and enhancing boundary perception. Low-rank adaptation (LoRA) [16] represents
another dominant PEFT paradigm, approximating weight updates via low-rank matrix
decomposition. Recent works like SAMed [9] apply LoRA to the query and value
projection layers of SAM’s image encoder, achieving competitive performance with
minimal trainable parameters.
Despite these advances, two fundamental limitations remain. First, existing methods
predominantly integrate local cues through post-hoc convolutional enhancement or
cross-branch fusion, rather than explicitly injecting local structural priors into the core
self-attention mechanism. As a result, their influence on the ViT’s long-range depen-
dency modeling remains suboptimal. To address this, we propose the convolutional
7


visual prompting (CVP) strategy, which explicitly prepends CNN-derived local priors to
the key and value sequences within multi-head self-attention. Second, most lightweight
modules prioritize generic low-dimensional feature transformations, lacking explicit
mechanisms to enhance high-frequency boundary information. This deficiency is par-
ticularly detrimental for medical images characterized by low contrast, blurred edges,
and subtle texture transitions. Meanwhile, recent frequency-domain analyses in founda-
tion models reveal that task-relevant semantics are usually encoded in high-frequency
features, while domain-specific biases reside in low-frequency components [40, 41].
Inspired by this, we design a boundary-aware adapter (BAA) that explicitly computes
and amplifies high-frequency components to improve feature representations along
lesion boundaries.
3. Method
3.1. Overall Framework
As illustrated in Fig. 2, our framework preserves the foundational SAM architecture
while systematically enhancing its image encoder to better capture local anatomical
structures and boundary details critical for medical image analysis. Inspired by the
dual-branch encoding paradigm of SAMUS [13], we adopt a parallel feature extraction
scheme comprising a ViT branch and a complementary CNN branch.
Given an input image of resolution H×W×3, where H and W denote the height and
width, respectively, both branches process the image in parallel. In the ViT branch, a
patch embedding layer projects the input into a sequence of tokens, establishing a feature
resolution of H/8×W/8, which remains consistent across all subsequent transformer
blocks. Meanwhile, the CNN branch employs a lightweight pathway consisting of one
SingleConv layer and three successive SingleConv-MaxPooling blocks, followed
by four additional SingleConv layers. Each SingleConv block comprises a 3×3
convolution, layer normalization, and a GELU activation. This design progressively
downsamples the feature maps and refines their representations, ultimately aligning
their spatial resolution with that of the ViT branch at H/8×W/8.

To address the inherent limitation of vanilla self-attention in capturing fine-grained
local details, we integrate two novel modules into specific global attention transformer
blocks. First, we introduce convolutional visual prompting (CVP), as shown in Fig. 3, a
mechanism that explicitly extracts local structural priors from the outputs of the final
SingleConv-MaxPooling block and the subsequent three SingleConv blocks in the
CNN branch. These priors are injected as visual prompts by being prepended to the key
and value projections within the multi-head self-attention (MHSA) layers of the 3-rd, 6-
th, 9-th, and 12-th ViT blocks, thereby enriching long-range dependency modeling with
structural cues. Second, we propose the boundary-aware adapter (BAA), as depicted in
Fig. 4, a parallel adaptation module inserted alongside the feed-forward network (FFN).
The BAA is specifically designed to compute and amplify high-frequency components
of the features, significantly enhancing the representation of tissue boundaries and other
subtle anatomical structures. Together, CVP and BAA enable the efficient adaptation of
SAM, leading to superior segmentation performance.

3.3. Boundary-Aware Adapter
As shown in Fig. 4, the boundary-aware adapter (BAA) is deployed as a parallel
branch alongside the original FFN, consisting of two essential modules: boundary
enhancement (BE) and a branch scaling factor s.
Boundary Enhancement. Inspired by the Deep Edge Filter [40], we design the
boundary enhancement (BE) module as an explicit high-pass filter constructed by
subtracting low-pass filtered outputs from input features. We implement the low-pass
filtering via a 3×3 average pooling operation. As depicted in Fig. 4 (left), given the
input features Xn ∈RN×C from layer normalization, we first project them into a low-
dimensional bottleneck space via a linear down projection layer followed by a GELU
activation:
Fb = GELU(XnWd),
(9)
where Fb ∈RN×C indicates the bottleneck features, Wd ∈RC×dr denotes the down
projection matrix, and dr ≪C is the bottleneck dimension. Then, we employ a 3×3
average pooling operation as the low-pass filter on Fb to yield the low-pass filtered
results Fl, and the generalizable, task-specific high-frequency representations Fh are
isolated by subtracting Fl from Fb:
Fl = AvgPool3×3(Fb),
Fh = Fb −Fl.
(10)

Subsequently, the bottleneck feature Fb and the extracted boundary component Fh are
concatenated along the channel dimension and fused through a 1×1 convolution:
Ff = Conv1×1 ([Fb; Fh]) .
(11)
Finally, Ff is activated by GELU and projected back to the original dimension via an
up-projection matrix Wu ∈Rdr×C, producing the BE Adapter (denoted as BEA) output:
BEA(Xn) = GELU(Ff )Wu.
(12)
Branch Scaling Factor s. Following prior work on adapter-based fine-tuning [21,
42, 43] and branch scaling [44], we introduce a scaling factor s to balance the contribu-
tion of the adapter branch relative to the frozen transformer pathway. The BE Adapter
output is integrated into the FFN via element-wise summation:
X′ = FFN(Xn) + s · BEA(Xn),
(13)
where FFN(·) denotes the original feed-forward network and X′ serves as the input
to subsequent transformer blocks. As a hyperparameter, s is helpful for the trade-off
between the primary semantic pathway and the high-frequency adapter branch, and thus
preventing optimization instability during early training stages. Our ablation studies
demonstrate that setting s = 0.3 yields optimal segmentation performance.

4. Experimental Settings
4.1. Datasets
To comprehensively evaluate the performance and generalization capability of
CVP-BAA, we conduct experiments on five publicly available datasets: BUSI [45],
UDIAT [46], TN3K [47], CVC-ClinicDB [48], and ISIC-2018 [49]. These datasets
span three representative medical imaging modalities: ultrasound, endoscopy, and der-
moscopy. Ultrasound imaging is particularly challenging due to inherent artifacts such
14


as low contrast, speckle noise, and ambiguous lesion boundaries, making it a rigorous
benchmark for assessing boundary-aware segmentation. Thus, we incorporate two
breast ultrasound datasets (BUSI and UDIAT) alongside one thyroid ultrasound dataset
(TN3K). Furthermore, to thoroughly assess cross-domain generalization, we include the
CVC-ClinicDB dataset for endoscopy and the ISIC-2018 dataset for dermoscopy. This
deliberately curated, multi-modality benchmark is designed to rigorously validate the
robustness and broad clinical applicability of the proposed adaptation method.
4.1.1. BUSI
The BUSI dataset contains 780 breast ultrasound images with pixel-wise annotations,
acquired using LOGIQ E9 and LOGIQ E9 Agile scanners. All the images have an
average resolution of 500×500 pixels in PNG format. To ensure fair comparison, we
adopt the same data split as SAMUS [13], dividing the dataset into training, validation,
and testing sets with a 7:1:2 ratio.
4.1.2. UDIAT
The UDIAT dataset comprises 163 pixel-wise annotated ultrasound images from
female patients, each containing one or more lesions. The images are acquired using
a Siemens ACUSON Sequoia C512 system with an 8.5 MHz 17L5 HD linear array
transducer and have a mean resolution of 760×570 pixels. For our experiments, the
dataset is partitioned into 115 training, 16 validation, and 32 testing images.
4.1.3. TN3K
The TN3K dataset comprises 3,493 thyroid ultrasound images with pixel-wise nod-
ule annotations, collected across three different ultrasound imaging systems: RESONA
70B, ARIETTA 850, and GE Logiq E9. Following the data partitioning protocol estab-
lished by SAMUS [13] and TRFE+ [47], we divide the dataset into 2,303 training, 576
validation, and 614 testing samples.
4.1.4. CVC-ClinicDB
The CVC-ClinicDB dataset consists of 612 colonoscopy images, originally utilized
as the benchmark for the MICCAI 2015 Sub-Challenge on Automatic Polyp Detection.
15


In our experiments, the dataset is randomly partitioned into 490 images for training, 61
for validation, and 61 for testing.
4.1.5. ISIC-2018
The ISIC-2018 dataset is widely recognized for benchmarking skin lesion segmenta-
tion methods. It comprises 2,594 clinical dermoscopy images with varying resolutions,
which are randomly partitioned into 1,868 training images, 467 validation images, and
259 testing images, respectively.
4.2. Evaluation Metrics
To ensure a fair comparison with state-of-the-art methods and to comprehensively
evaluate the performance of CVP-BAA, we employ six standard segmentation metrics:
Dice coefficient (Dice), Hausdorff distance (HD), mean Intersection over Union (mIoU),
Accuracy (Acc), Precision (Prec), and Recall (Rec). For the ultrasound datasets: BUSI,
UDIAT, and TN3K, we report Dice and HD; for ISIC-2018 and CVC-ClinicDB, we
report Dice, mIoU, Acc, Prec, and Rec. Additionally, we assess computational efficiency
through the number of parameters (#Params), floating-point operations (FLOPs), and
inference speed (FPS). All results are reported as mean±standard deviation over three in-
dependent runs with different random seeds to verify the robustness and generalizability
of the proposed CVP-BAA.
4.3. Implementation Details
We adopt the pre-trained SAM with a ViT-B backbone, originally trained on the
SA-1B dataset, as our foundational architecture. All three components in this version of
SAM, namely, the image encoder, prompt encoder, and mask decoder, remain frozen
during training. Only the parameters of the parallel CNN branch, along with the CVP
and BAA components, are optimized. All experiments are conducted on an NVIDIA
GeForce RTX 3090 GPU with 24 GB of memory. The models are trained for 200 epochs
using the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 1e −4 and a batch size of 8.
The training objective is supervised by a hybrid loss function combining Dice loss and
binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss. In addition, following the protocol of SAMUS [13],
16


we employ a single-point prompt for interaction, and all input images are uniformly
resized to 256×256 pixels.
5. Experimental Results
5.1. Comparisons with other methods
We evaluate CVP-BAA against a comprehensive suite of state-of-the-art baselines.
This includes representative task-specific architectures spanning CNN-based models
like UNet [2] and AAU-Net [23], pure transformer frameworks such as Swin-Unet [3]
and MISSFormer [24], and hybrid CNN-Transformer models including TransFuse [4],
H2Former [26], and BRAU-Net++ [5]. Additionally, we benchmark our method against
leading SAM adaptation strategies, namely vanilla SAM [7], MedSAM [12], SAMed [9],
and Med-SA [8].

Dice scores across all three benchmarks, reaching 86.71%, 89.72%, and 85.23% on

17


extraction of CNNs with the global context modeling of Transformers, they remain

The integration of solely the CVP component into the baseline architecture leads to
consistent and substantial segmentation gains: +2.12% in Dice and -1.97 mm in HD

lesion localization.

Integrating the BAA component alone into the baseline yields consistent perfor-
mance improvements: achieving a +3.58% gain in Dice and a -2.83 mm reduction in
HD on the BUSI dataset, alongside a +3.09% gain in Dice and a -1.97 mm reduction
in HD on the TN3K dataset. This indicates that incorporating the BAA significantly
boosts performance, confirming its specific effectiveness in enhancing high-frequency
boundary delineation and reducing boundary errors.

Combining both CVP and BAA achieves the best performance across both datasets,
yielding 86.71% Dice and 24.52 mm HD on BUSI, alongside 85.23% Dice and 27.77

enabling a highly effective adaptation of SAM for medical image segmentation.

detailed in Section 3.2, we prepend the visual prompt P to the K and V sequences. An
alternative approach is to add the prompts element-wise to K and V, which preserves
the original sequence length of the transformer. The quantitative results, presented
in Table 5, demonstrate that our default sequence prepending strategy consistently

of 0.78% in Dice and -1.05 mm in HD on BUSI, and 0.49% in Dice and -0.38 mm in
HD on TN3K. Consequently, we adopt the sequence prepending scheme for our final
architecture.

5.2.7. Visualization of Component Contributions
To provide qualitative insights into the internal representations learned by each
proposed component, we visualize the attention maps from the final block of the
SAM image encoder using Grad-CAM [50], as illustrated in Fig. 8. The attention
distribution of the baseline model is highly dispersed and frequently focuses on irrelevant
background regions, leading to inaccurate lesion localization. In contrast, incorporating
the CVP enhances attention on local, fine-grained structures, such as the spiculated
margins of malignant breast tumors in BUSI or the subtle textural variations of thyroid
nodules in TN3K. This demonstrates that our local CNN features successfully guide the
27


global attention mechanism. Meanwhile, the integration of the BAA explicitly activates
high-frequency boundary regions, directly validating our design motivation of isolating
edge cues via high-pass filtering. Finally, the full CVP-BAA model integrates these
complementary strengths, refining the overall attention distribution to produce the most
precise and boundary-aligned activation maps, thereby providing compelling visual
evidence of our framework’s effectiveness.
5.3. Efficiency Analysis
We further evaluate the computational efficiency of the compared SAM-based
models in terms of trainable parameters (#Train. Params), total parameters (#Total
Params), computational complexity (GFLOPs), and inference speed (FPS). To ensure
a fair comparison, all metrics are measured on the BUSI dataset using a uniform
input resolution of 256×256 under identical hardware configurations, as summarized in
Table 8. Compared to the strongest baseline, SAMUS, CVP-BAA significantly improves
parameter efficiency by reducing the trainable parameter count from 39.65M to 30.20M,
a ∼23.8% reduction, while maintaining a smaller total parameter scale. Furthermore,
CVP-BAA achieves a highly competitive computational footprint, requiring only 143.46
GFLOPs and delivering a robust inference speed of 37.92 FPS. Although requiring more
trainable parameters (with 30.20M) than SAMed (with 18.81M), CVP-BAA achieves
a superior trade-off between parameter efficiency and model capacity, maintaining
comparable inference speed while yielding substantially better segmentation accuracy,
as shown in Table 1. These results demonstrate that CVP-BAA enhances adaptation
effectiveness without introducing excessive computational overhead.
6. Conclusion
In this paper, we propose CVP-BAA, an efficient SAM adaptation framework for
medical image segmentation. The proposed convolutional visual prompting (CVP)
strategy explicitly injects local structural priors into the global dependency modeling.
Furthermore, the boundary-aware adapter (BAA) significantly enhances the representa-
tion of fine-grained structures and ambiguous lesion boundaries. Extensive experiments
