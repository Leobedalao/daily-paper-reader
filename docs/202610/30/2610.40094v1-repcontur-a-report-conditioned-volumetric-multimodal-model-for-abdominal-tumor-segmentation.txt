## Abstract 

Tumor delineation is required for radiotherapy planning, yet automatic segmentation is limited by errors, predomi-nantly false positives, that distort dose and increase edit-ing time. The radiology report accompanies the scan and localizes disease at the suborgan level, but exist-ing text-promptable models condition on short referring phrases, and most operate in 2D. We present RepCon-TUR, a single native-3D early-fusion multimodal model that conditions volumetric abdominal tumor segmentation on the narrative report. We extend AbdomenAtlas 3.0 to a suborgan-level promptable segmentation dataset and gen-erate report-based VQA pairs, yielding four report-derived tasks on the same annotations: referring segmentation (RES), report-grounded segmentation (GSEG), report gen-eration (RRG), and visual question answering (VQA). We train RepConTUR, comprising a 3D CT encoder, an early-fusion language model, and a native-3D segmentation mod-ule, jointly on the four tasks. Across liver, kidney and pan-creas, RepConTUR attains the highest Dice similarity co-efficient (DSC), instance-wise precision, and detection F1 among the text-promptable baselines, and adding the re-port raises mean precision while leaving recall compara-ble. Against two vision-only specialists, nnU-Net and the report-supervised R-Super, RepConTUR attains the highest value on every in-domain metric for liver and pancreas and the highest mean DSC, recall, precision, and detection F1 over the four out-of-distribution public cohorts. Ablations indicate that conditioning on the narrative report raises de-tection F1 from 0.499 to 0.528 within the same model, that joint GSEG training raises RES F1 from 0.450 to 0.482, and that the native-3D segmentation module raises GSEG F1 from 0.139 to 0.528 over its 2D counterpart. Code is available at github.com/Oncosoft-Research/RepConTUR.

## 1. Introduction 

Tumor delineation is a required step in radiotherapy plan-ning, but manual contouring is labor-intensive and shows large inter-observer variability [21, 52, 58]. Unlike diagnos-tic reading, radiotherapy target contouring routinely com-bines the diagnostic scan with its radiology report, as the report contains rich information about the tumor, including its anatomical location, size and stage [1, 7, 20, 29]. This makes the report a natural conditioning signal for automatic tumor segmentation. Vision-only deep learning models remain the standard approach to tumor segmentation, and their accuracy is re-ported mainly by the volumetric Dice similarity coefficient (DSC), which underrepresents instance-wise recall and pre-cision [37]. On the LiTS liver-tumor benchmark, the top method reports a tumor Dice of 0.702 , yet its instance-wise precision is only 0.148 [6]. In radiotherapy, instance-wise recall and precision are as clinically important as DSC. False positives in particular distort the dose distribu-tion and raise normal-tissue exposure and toxicity risk [44], and these errors increase the editing time [57]. A sepa-rate line of work uses the radiology report as training-time supervision to improve tumor detection and segmentation. Report-derived tumor count and size penalize inconsistent predictions through auxiliary losses [3], reported location provides weak labels [23], and the report yields pseudo-labels [9]. However, these models still segment from the image alone at inference time [64], so the report cannot di-rectly inform the prediction. Text-guided segmentation models have achieved strong results on referring segmentation (RES) and grounded seg-mentation (GSEG) [31, 33, 45, 63, 76]. Some works adopt a dual-encoder design that encodes the language prompt and the image in separate branches and fuses them at the de-coder [47, 70]. On the other hand, multimodal large lan-guage model (MLLM)-based segmentation models fuse im-age and text early and reason over both jointly within the 1arXiv:2610.40094v1 [cs.CV] 29 Oct 2026 language model [31, 61]. Yet both are designed for short target descriptions — class names or referring phrases — and do not exploit long-form textual context. BiomedParse v2, for example, conditions on an organ-level prompt (e.g., “tumor in liver CT”), which cannot indicate a detailed intra-organ region and may not capture suborgan-level informa-tion [70, 71]. A report instead specifies suborgan location, which may help suppress false positives elsewhere in the same organ [17, 18]. Furthermore, existing MLLM-based medical segmentation models are also mostly 2D [61], lim-iting their abilities to interpret report information that refers to 3D context [11, 30, 38]. Multitask learning can improve a target task when the tasks are related, although the gain depends on task affin-ity [10, 50, 55]. Unified vision–language models train local-ization and language tasks in one network [59, 68], and an MLLM-based segmentation model is a natural fit for such joint training. In the medical domain, UniBioMed trains segmentation jointly with VQA and report generation and reports improved segmentation Dice [61]. The report can therefore also serve as a joint-training signal. We propose RepConTUR, an MLLM-based 3D report-conditioned tumor segmentation model that grounds seg-mentation on the report to improve both segmentation accu-racy and instance-wise detection (Fig. 1). Our contributions are threefold: • Report-conditioned segmentation. We condition seg-mentation on a suborgan-level referring phrase and an op-tional free-form narrative report, rather than a short class name alone. To the best of our knowledge, RepConTUR is the first 3D referring/grounded segmentation model to condition on such report context. We introduce suborgan-level mask and prompt design aligned to narrative free-form radiology reports, and implement it for abdominal tumors. • A native 3D MLLM for report-conditioned abdominal tumor segmentation. We build on prior MLLM archi-tectures and extend them with native-3D vision modules. The MLLM vision encoder and segmentation module are all 3D, and visual tokens are processed jointly with the report text from the first LLM layer, so vision and text are reasoned over jointly. • Report-derived multitask dataset and multitask learn-ing. From AbdomenAtlas 3.0 [4] tumor masks and their reports we construct a unified four-task dataset. Report-matched suborgan localization produces the RES and GSEG targets, and the report is converted into radiology report generation (RRG) supervision and LLM-verified VQA pairs. We jointly train all four tasks on a single backbone. Cumulative task ablations show that report-grounded segmentation training improves RES perfor-mance, while adding report generation and VQA further increases detection F1. 

## 2. Related work 

2.1. Reports and tumor segmentation 

One line of work uses the report as a training signal. R-Super converts report-derived tumor count and size into voxel-wise losses [3], Guo et al . use report text as weak supervision to improve detection [23], Bosma et al . derive pseudo-labels from the report [9], and SGSeg uses text only during training and drops it at inference [64]. These methods all use the report only at training time. In contrast, we condition 3D tumor segmentation on the narra-tive report at inference. 

2.2. Text-promptable medical segmentation 

These methods share a common pipeline. A separate text encoder maps a short query to an embedding that is fused with vision features, usually late at the decoder, to produce a text-conditioned mask. The two families differ only in the query. Open-vocabulary methods use a class name or anatomical term as the query (SegVol [19], SAT [72], CLIP-Universal [36], BiomedParse v2 [70, 71]), while a parallel line uses a referring or descriptive phrase (LViT [35], Ari-adne’s Thread [73], RecLMIS [27], CausalCLIPSeg [15], LSMS [43], VoxTell [47]) or attribute labels such as tumor count and severity (TGANet [56], Bi-VLGM [14]). These methods primarily target class-level or phrase-level queries, leaving their effectiveness with full narrative reports less established. We investigate report conditioning by jointly processing volumetric visual tokens and report text in an MLLM before mask prediction. 

2.3. MLLM-based segmentation 

This family splits into two modules. An MLLM processes text and image jointly to produce an embedding aligned to the image, and a separate segmentation module combines that embedding with fine-grained backbone features to pro-duce the mask. LISA introduced this design for natural im-ages [31] and later works extended it [45, 60, 62, 67]. Amedical branch adapts this design to biomedical images, usually in 2D. UniBioMed [61] couples an MLLM with a SAM-style segmentation module for grounded biomedi-cal interpretation. MedPLIB [28] drives a separate SAM-Med mask decoder from a [SEG] embedding, with the pixel-grounding path routed through an in-LLM mixture-of-experts. RepConTUR inherits this aligned embedding plus sepa-rate segmentation module design, but extends the 2D medi-cal setting to full 3D and broadens the conditioning from a short referring phrase to the narrative report.

We jointly train all four on full-3D CT volumes and condition segmentation on the narrative report. 

## 3. Report-guided multitask dataset 

We build the report-guided multitask dataset from Abdom-enAtlas 3.0 [4], which provides 9,262 abdominal CT vol-umes with tumor masks for liver, kidney and pancreas, and organ masks that include suborgan structures such as liver Couinaud segments. Each volume also carries a synthet-ically generated radiology report from the RadGPT con-struction pipeline [4]. A structured report is first derived deterministically from the masks, listing per suborgan the tumor count, size, location, and attenuation. A large lan-guage model then rewrites the structured report into a nar-rative free-text report in radiologist style. The narrative may not enumerate every tumor. For multifocal disease it often reports the largest tumor with its suborgan and an aggregate tumor count rather than each tumor’s location. Across the 

9,262 volumes, the narrative reports have a mean sequence length of 225 tokens and a maximum of 1,163 tokens under the Clinical-Longformer tokenizer [34]. We exclude volumes without tumors in any of the three organs to match the clinical setting of radiotherapy, where planning is performed only for tumor-positive pa-tients. Of the 9,262 volumes, 5,464 are tumor-free and re-moved, leaving 3,798 tumor-bearing volumes for training and evaluation. Positives are unevenly distributed across organs, with 1,508 liver-positive, 2,222 kidney-positive and 

1,113 pancreas-positive volumes, namely 39 .7% , 58 .5% 

and 29 .3% . Every organ is therefore trained with both tumor-present and tumor-absent volumes. 

Report-guided tumor localization. The original tumor masks are at organ level, so we decompose them into per-suborgan instances using the structured report. We extract the connected components (CC) of the tumor mask. For each component c and suborgan mask s we measure their intersection over component, 

IoC( c, s ) = |c ∩ s||c| , (1) and associate c with every suborgan whose IoC is nonzero, since one component can span more than one sub-3organ. The structured report describes the tumors at two levels of location, and we assign components differently for the two. Most findings name the suborgan and state how many tumors it contains, whereas others give only an organ-level finding without a suborgan. For a suborgan named by the report, we rank its overlapping components by IoC and keep the highest-ranked ones up to the tumor count stated for that suborgan. For a tumor the report places only at the organ level, the component is assigned to every suborgan whose IoC exceeds a fixed threshold and to its maximum-IoC suborgan. A component that overlaps no suborgan is instead assigned by spatial proximity. Because a suborgan can hold several tumors, we merge all components assigned to it into a single mask by voxel-wise union. 

VQA generation. We generate up to 20 question– answer pairs per volume from the structured and narra-tive reports in a generation stage followed by a verification stage. The structured report supplies the exact per-tumor facts queried, namely suborgan location, size, and attenu-ation, while the narrative supplies tumor staging and clin-ical interpretation such as resectability. In the generation stage, a large language model writes pairs in three answer formats, namely binary yes/no, short answer, and multiple choice with enumerated options. The pairs span tumor pres-ence, tumor count, suborgan location, tumor size, enhance-ment pattern relative to the organ, and organ-level findings such as organ enlargement. Tumor size is queried as an interval rather than an exact measurement, since an exact diameter varies with the measurer while a coarse band is re-producible, using the bands < 1, 1–2, 2–3, 3–5, 5–10 and 

> 10 cm. “No” questions are generated by perturbing a stated finding into a plausible but incorrect variation such as a different suborgan, side, or stage. In the verification stage, a separate language model checks each pair against the source structured and narrative reports and corrects or discards any pair that is unsupported by the reports, not an-swerable from the image alone, malformed in its answer, insufficiently localized, or whose negative is not a plausible variation. Across the 3,798 tumor-bearing volumes, veri-fication retains 71 ,783 pairs, 18 .9 per volume on average, comprising 48 ,451 yes/no, 13 ,433 short-answer and 9,899 

multiple-choice. Within the yes/no pairs, 31 ,449 (64 .9% )are affirmative and 17 ,002 (35 .1% ) negative. 

Multitask composition. We train RepConTUR jointly on four objectives, namely RES and GSEG for segmen-tation, RRG for radiology report generation and VQA for visual question answering. RES conditions on a suborgan-level referring phrase drawn from a pool of phrasal variants that indicate the tumor in a suborgan, for example “tumor in hepatic segment 7”. At training time one phrase is sam-pled from this pool and inserted into a randomly sampled instruction template, such as “Please segment the tumor in {phrase }.” or “Please delineate the GTV in {phrase }.”. RES also includes a whole-organ target that aggregates ev-ery tumor in an organ, named by an organ-level phrase such as “total liver GTV” or “all tumors in the kidney”. GSEG reuses the same target mask and referring phrase but prepends the full narrative report to the instruction, such as “{report } . . . Based on the above findings, please segment the tumor in {phrase }.”. The comparison therefore mea-sures the additional benefit of report context beyond the tar-get phrase, with the image and requested target held fixed. RRG generates the report from the CT, and VQA an-swers the report-derived questions. The two language tasks supply language supervision to the shared backbone that the segmentation tasks reuse. 

## 4. 3D MLLM segmentation model 

Early-fusion 3D MLLM. We adopt the CT-ViT of CT-CLIP [24] as the 3D visual encoder. It factorizes atten-tion into a spatial transformer that attends within each axial plane followed by a temporal transformer that attends across planes along the through-slice axis, producing a 3D token grid. This encodes the whole volume in 3D at lower cost than full 3D attention. We vary the original implementa-tion in its input, resampling volumes to a (1 .5, 1.5, 3.0) mm spacing and a 240 × 240 × 140 grid. We pretrain the en-coder with a CLIP objective, aligning each CT volume em-bedding with a Clinical-Longformer [34] embedding of its report. The original CT-ViT is pretrained only on chest CT from CT-RATE [24], so we add abdominal CT image– report pairs from Merlin [8] to generalize the encoder from chest to abdominal radiological semantics, which our down-stream abdominal tasks require. After pretraining we keep the frozen image branch as a feature extractor. Following CoCa [66], an attentional pooler with 256 learnable queries cross-attends to the encoder tokens to pro-duce 256 fixed-length visual tokens, projected to the LLM hidden dimension. We adopt a InternVL2.5-1B [16] lan-guage model as the backbone, initialized from the medically pretrained UniBioMed [61] weights. The LLM is frozen and adapted with LoRA [26]. We introduce a [SEG] token for requesting the segmentation [31]. 

3D segmentation module. We adopt VoxTell [47] as the 3D segmentation module, initialized from its pretrained weights. It pairs an nnU-Net residual encoder [30] with a text-prompted decoder that consists of a prompt transformer and a U-Net decoder. We keep the encoder frozen and train the decoder. We construct this query from the language model. The hidden state of the [SEG] token is mapped by a two-layer projection to a 2048 -dimensional query embedding. Be-cause the MLLM fuses image and text early, this embedding jointly encodes the volume and the input text. The prompt transformer cross-attends from it to the encoder features to form a mask embedding, which the U-Net decoder fuses at 4every scale to produce the full-resolution volumetric mask. 

## 5. Experiments and results 

5.1. Setup 

We evaluate abdominal tumor delineation on liver, kidney and pancreas across two streams, plus design ablations. 

Text-promptable comparison. The first stream eval-uates text-promptable segmentation under a single refer-ring prompt, where RepConTUR is driven either by RES mode or GSEG mode. We compare it against three text-promptable baselines, namely the 2D MLLM-based model UniBioMed [61], the 2D late-fusion model BiomedParse v2 [70, 71], and the 3D late-fusion model VoxTell [47]. We finetune each baseline on our dataset with its own fine-tuning recipe, restricted to the tasks it supports, namely BiomedParse v2 on RES, VoxTell on RES and GSEG, and UniBioMed on all four tasks. This stream is evaluated on the AbdomenAtlas 3.0 test split. 

Report-guided organ-wise segmentation. The second stream evaluates report-guided organ-wise segmentation against two vision-only specialists, the nnU-Net special-ist [30] trained to segment liver, kidney and pancreas tumors jointly as separate classes, and R-Super [3], a segmenta-tion model supervised by radiology reports during training; both use no report at inference. To simulate the radiother-apy setting where a CT scan and its report are available to-gether, we run a simple agentic inference pipeline in which an LLM parses the report into the tumor-bearing suborgans, RepConTUR grounds and segments each suborgan under RES or GSEG, and the per-suborgan masks are combined by union into an organ-wise prediction. When the narrative report aggregates findings at the organ level and does not localize the tumor to a specific suborgan, the LLM cannot name a suborgan target, and we prompt RepConTUR with a single whole-organ tumor phrase for that organ. Because neither specialist can ground individual suborgans, the re-port enters them only as an organ-level restriction that ze-roes any organ the report marks tumor-free. This stream is evaluated on an in-domain test set and four external out-of-distribution (OOD) sets, namely HCC-TACE-Seg for pri-mary hepatocellular carcinoma [39, 40], Colorectal-Liver-Metastases for colorectal liver metastases [53, 54], CPTAC-PDA for pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma [42, 49], and CPTAC-CCRCC for clear cell renal cell carcinoma [41, 48]. These OOD sets have no radiology reports, so for each case we synthesize one with the same construction pipeline used for AbdomenAtlas 3.0 [4] and use it both to drive the agen-tic inference pipeline and as the GSEG grounding text.

5.5. Limitations and future work 

RepConTUR targets three abdominal organs, namely liver, kidney and pancreas, while tumors in the other body regions are excluded in this work. Whether the approach scales to a larger set of tumor classes, spanning further abdominal or-gans and other body regions, is not established, and verify-ing this class-scaling ability is a primary direction for future work. The validation cohorts lack real reports and were grounded with synthetic ones. ## 6. Conclusion 

We presented RepConTUR, a native-3D multimodal model that conditions abdominal tumor segmentation on a refer-ring phrase and, optionally, a narrative report, using report-aligned suborgan masks. A report-derived dataset supports joint training on four tasks from AbdomenAtlas 3.0, namely referring and grounded segmentation, report generation and question answering. RepConTUR attains the highest DSC, precision and detection F1 among the text-promptable base-lines, and improves mean detection F1 over the nnU-Net and R-Super specialists in-domain and across four exter-nal cohorts, although performance varies by organ. These results support report-conditioned segmentation under syn-thetic reports, and validation with independently authored 8clinical reports remains necessary.

A.1. Method and implementation details 

A.1.1. Training and inference 

This section details the CT preprocessing, training and in-ference pipelines. 

Target construction. The decomposition of Sec. 3 uses a fixed IoC threshold of 0.2 for a component the report places only at the organ level. Spatial proximity is mea-sured as the surface distance from a component to a sub-organ. The surface-distance rule also fills a stated slot when a suborgan’s reported tumor count exceeds its over-lapping components. Across the 14 ,584 tumor components, the suborgan-count step accounts for 11 ,958 and the organ-level rule handles the remaining 2,618 , while IoC assigns 

13 ,723 and surface distance assigns 853 . Splitting the two jointly, 80 .4% of components are suborgan-stated and as-signed by IoC, 1.6% suborgan-stated by surface distance, 

13 .7% organ-level by IoC, and 4.3% organ-level by surface distance. 

CT preprocessing. The language encoder and the seg-mentation module follow different pipelines. The language encoder consumes a fixed 240 × 240 × 140 volume, pro-duced by resampling to (1 .5, 1.5, 3.0) mm and then center-cropping or padding, clipped to [−1000 , 1000] HU and min–max normalized to [−1, 1] . The segmentation module trains on 1 mm isotropic, z-score-normalized 192 3 patches. The segmentation volume is first padded so every dimen-sion is at least 192 , and a 192 3 patch is then drawn per volume by a foreground-biased crop that samples tumor-positive and tumor-negative centers in equal proportion. Aside from the random crop location, no data augmentation is applied. 

Losses. Segmentation uses voxel-wise sigmoid cross-entropy and Dice loss summed 1:1 , and the language tasks use standard language-model cross-entropy. The two are summed according to the task composition of each batch. 

Optimization. RepConTUR trains for 20 epochs on four NVIDIA H100 80GB GPUs in bf16 mixed precision. Dis-tributed training uses the DeepSpeed ZeRO-2 strategy, with a per-GPU batch of 4 and gradient accumulation of 8, giving an effective batch of 128 and a maximum sequence length of 8192 . The optimizer is AdamW ( β=(0 .9, 0.999) , weight decay 0.05 ) with learning rate 4×10 −5 and gradient norms clipped to 1, using one epoch of linear warmup at ratio 0.05 

followed by cosine annealing. The LoRA adapter has rank 

64 and α=128 .

VQA generation. The VQA pairs are generated with GPT-5.4 and verified with GPT-5.4-mini, which corrects or discards pairs inconsistent with the report. 

Inference. At inference RepConTUR segments the full volume by sliding-window inference with a 192 3 window and 50% overlap, caching the language-model features of the fixed-grid volume and prompt so they are computed once and reused across suborgan prompts, as detailed in Sec. A.4.1. 

A.1.2. Out-of-distribution sets Cohort construction and exclusion criteria. The four out-of-distribution cohorts (Tab. 5) are built from pub-lic TCIA collections, namely HCC-TACE-Seg [39, 40], Colorectal-Liver-Metastases [53, 54], CPTAC-PDA [42, 49] and CPTAC-CCRCC [41, 48]. The four cohorts span three organs and both primary and metastatic disease, and each targets a distinct clinical scenario. HCC-TACE-Seg contains primary hepatocellular carcinoma imaged in a transarterial-chemoembolization cohort, so it probes delin-eation of primary liver tumors whose appearance is altered by locoregional therapy. Colorectal-Liver-Metastases con-tains multifocal colorectal liver metastases, so it probes sen-sitivity to several scattered deposits rather than to a sin-gle tumor. CPTAC-PDA contains pancreatic ductal ade-nocarcinoma, an infiltrative and low-contrast primary, so it probes the hardest abdominal target, where small tumors are the primary detection challenge. CPTAC-CCRCC contains clear cell renal cell carcinoma and probes primary renal-tumor delineation, the organ on which the vision-only spe-cialist was strongest in domain. The evaluation unit is the CT series rather than the pa-tient, so a patient with several eligible contrast phases con-tributes several scans. The HCC-TACE-Seg CT series in-terleaves two to three contrast phases, so we separate the phases by acquisition and keep every phase that contains the tumor, yielding 130 scans from 105 patients. CPTAC-PDA and CPTAC-CCRCC likewise yield 108 scans from 

67 and 47 patients, and Colorectal-Liver-Metastases con-tributes one scan per patient for 197 scans. We exclude contours drawn on MR or PET series and discard any scan whose contours do not rasterize to a non-empty tumor mask on the CT grid. 

Synthetic report generation. Because the four cohorts provide no radiology reports, we synthesize a report for each scan with the same construction pipeline used for Ab-domenAtlas 3.0 [4]. Because these sets also lack organ an-notations, we first obtain multi-organ and suborgan masks with a pretrained MedFormer segmentation model [4] and pair them with each set’s ground-truth tumor masks. From the combined masks a structured report is derived determin-istically by the same construction as RadGPT [4], listing per suborgan the tumor count, size, location and attenuation, as in Sec. 3. OpenAI’s gpt-5.6-terra then rewrites the structured report into a narrative free-text report in the AbdomenAtlas 3.0 style, using the same system prompt as RadGPT [4]. 13 A.2. We compare inference cost against the two vision-only specialists, nnU-Net and R-Super. All models run on the same GPU, an NVIDIA RTX A5000, on a single in-domain test volume. We time the network sliding-window pass and average over repeated runs, with a per-value standard devi-ation below 0.1 s. The volume is the most tumor-involved case in the cohort, with 7 suborgan targets across all three organs, and approximates the upper end of RepConTUR cost. Both specialists run a single vision-only sliding-window pass. For RepConTUR the measurement exercises its inference caching. The multimodal encoder computes the image features once and memoizes them for reuse across all suborgan prompts. The report prefix, namely the image tokens and, for GSEG, the narrative report, is prefilled once into the language-model key-value cache. It is reused for every suborgan prompt, which differs only in the trailing target phrase. The segmentation branch encodes the 192 3

sliding window once per call and shares it across targets, de-coding one mask per suborgan from the common features. RES and GSEG differ only in the prefill length. Table A.7 shows that RES and GSEG have the same measured infer-ence time in this setting. nnU-Net is fastest at 6.2 s, and RepConTUR and R-Super are comparable at 23 .7 s and 24 .6 s, with R-Super timed under GPU-side window aggregation. R-Super and RepConTUR run a 1 mm isotropic sliding window over the finer 324 × 249 × 628 grid, with 128 3 and 192 3

patches. nnU-Net’s self-configured plan instead resam-ples to a coarser 0.86 × 0.86 × 2.0 mm spacing, giving a 

376 × 289 × 314 grid tiled with a 256 × 192 × 112 patch, so it evaluates substantially fewer windows. The RepCon-TUR cost decomposes into a one-time encoding and a per-suborgan decode. The multimodal image encoding is neg-ligible at 0.01 s. The grounding sliding-window encoding over the 1 mm grid costs approximately 5 s and is computed once per volume. Each suborgan target then adds approxi-mately 2.7 s for its own mask decode, so latency grows lin-early with the number of targets, from 7.6 s for one target to 23 .7 s for all seven. 18
