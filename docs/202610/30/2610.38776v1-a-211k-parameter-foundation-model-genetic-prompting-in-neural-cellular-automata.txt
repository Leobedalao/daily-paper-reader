Abstract
The scale of modern foundation models enables broad task generalization. In
medical imaging, where segmentation demands vary widely across clinical contexts,
such flexibility is invaluable. But current models, such as SAM trade adaptability
for scale, locking their utility behind high-end infrastructure and leaving behind
the low-resource environments most in need of diagnostic support. To bridge this
gap, we introduce Foundational-NCA, a lightweight foundation model capable of
adapting to a wide range of medical segmentation tasks without retraining. Built
on Neural Cellular Automata (NCA), Foundational-NCA incorporates gradient-
based learning with genetic optimization over a latent task space, using a compact
task vector to guide predictions. This enables dynamic adaptation via a genetic
prompt mechanism, allowing the model to generalize across tasks while keeping the
parameter count magnitudes lower at just 211 thousand, compared to SAM-based
models with 38.9 to 636 million parameters. We evaluate Foundational-NCA on
15 trained and 6 entirely unseen segmentation tasks from the TotalSegmentator
dataset, achieving an average Dice score of 84.5% on known tasks and 60.4%
on unseen ones. These results demonstrate that task generalization is possible in
ultra-compact models, laying the groundwork for resource-efficient, deployable
foundation models in clinical environments where infrastructure is limited. By
reducing model size by over 180 times, Foundational-NCA takes a step toward a
more equitable future for medical AI, one where powerful tools are within reach of
every healthcare setting.

Introduction
The rise of foundation models has marked a shift from task-specific tools to scalable, unified
systems capable of broad generalization. In medical imaging, this versatility promises transformative
improvements in diagnostics and patient care [29], enabling accurate, scalable segmentation across
clinical contexts. Yet, the immense computational scale of these systems, typically involving hundreds
of millions of parameters, restricts their deployment to high-resource environments [1]. Consequently,
their substantial benefits remain inaccessible precisely in regions where advanced AI could make the
greatest difference: low-resource healthcare settings, such as low and middle-income countries with
critical shortages of medical personnel [4, 7, 16, 18, 21].
This disparity has profound implications for global health equity. Accurate and accessible AI-driven
segmentation can directly influence patient outcomes, underpinning critical decisions from early
disease detection to surgical planning [15, 26, 32]. However, current state-of-the-art solutions, such as
the Segment Anything Model (SAM) [13] and its medically-adapted variant MedSAM [15], remain
impractically large, requiring between 38.9 and 636 million parameters. This complexity makes their
deployment on widely available, cost-effective hardware such as smartphones infeasible, particularly

Neural Cellular Automata (NCAs) [6, 20], inspired by self-organizing biological systems, present a
promising alternative due to their minimal resource requirements [9], low parameter count [2, 8, 11,
19, 23, 24] and inherent adaptability [10]. Recent advancements, such as Med-NCA [8, 10], have
shown that NCA can achieve strong segmentation performance with significantly fewer resources.
However, current NCA-based segmentation models are fundamentally limited: they are designed for
single-task scenarios and lack the flexibility required to handle multiple tasks dynamically.
In this work, we introduce Foundational-NCA, the first NCA-based foundation model capable of
adapting dynamically across multiple segmentation tasks via prompt-based conditioning. Central to
our approach is a compact, continuous task vector, optimized using genetic algorithms (GA) [27] to
encode specific segmentation objectives. This task vector modulates the NCA’s update dynamics at
each step, allowing a single fixed model to perform diverse segmentation tasks without any weight
modifications (Figure 1). By integrating genetic optimization for task conditioning with gradient-
based training of model parameters, Foundational-NCA generalizes effectively across 15 trained
anatomical targets and demonstrates promising adaptability to 6 entirely unseen segmentation tasks,
delivering foundation-level versatility with only 211K parameters.
Evaluated on the TotalSegmentator dataset [31], Foundational-NCA achieves an average Dice score
of 84.5% across 15 training tasks and 60.4% on 6 unseen tasks, using only 211K parameters. In
comparison, MedSAM, a 93-million-parameter foundation model, attains 75.8% and 73.4% on the
same task sets. While Foundational-NCA shows a trade-off in generalization to unseen targets, it
outperforms MedSAM on seen tasks with over 400× fewer parameters, highlighting the potential of
compact, prompt-driven models for low-resource scenarios.
By substantially reducing hardware requirements and demonstrating a first step toward generalization
within an ultra-lightweight foundation model, Foundational-NCA offers a promising direction for
democratizing medical AI. It provides an initial framework toward universally accessible foundation
models, compact, adaptive, and with the potential to bring advanced healthcare solutions closer to
everyone, everywhere.

Related Work
This work builds upon two key areas: genetic optimization, a class of gradient-free search algorithms
well-suited to non-differentiable objectives, and neural cellular automata, a paradigm for self-
organizing, locally updated neural networks.

2.2
Neural Cellular Automata
Neural Cellular Automata (NCA) are models characterized by local communication and self-
organization, first introduced by [20]. They build upon the concept of classical Cellular Automata,
originally proposed by von Neumann [30] in the 1940s, where complex global behavior emerges from
simple local interactions. Unlike traditional Cellular Automata, which rely on hand-crafted update
rules, NCA replace these rules with a learnable, fully convolutional neural network with pixel-based
dropout, that governs the state updates of each cell based solely on local neighborhood information.
The model evolves iteratively, applying the learned update rule over multiple time steps to reach a
predefined target state. However, early applications of NCA were largely confined to toy problems due
to the high memory demands of storing state trajectories during training and slow local communication.
This bottleneck was alleviated by the work of [8], who introduced a memory-efficient training
procedure that allows NCA to be applied to more complex and practical tasks.
3
Methodology
We introduce Foundational-NCA, a compact NCA architecture conditioned on a learned task vector,
enabling segmentation of multiple anatomical targets without weight modification. This is achieved
through a combination of gradient-based training and genetic optimization.
3.1
Backbone
We build on the Med-NCA architecture [8], which enhances NCA for high-resolution medical image
segmentation by addressing two key challenges: (1) the inability of standard NCAs to capture both
global context and fine spatial detail, and (2) the high memory cost of storing recurrent NCA states
during training. Med-NCA introduces a multi-scale hierarchical design, where a coarse-resolution
model m1 processes a downsampled image, and a fine-resolution model m2 refines the output using
both high-resolution input and coarse features. This design enables the model to reason over global
structures while preserving fine anatomical boundaries. Additionally, a randomized patch-based
training strategy greatly reduces GPU memory requirements. We refer readers to the Med-NCA
papers [8, 10] for full architectural and implementation details.

3.2
Prompting NCAs
In Foundational-NCA, we extend Med-NCA by incorporating two conditioning prompts: a task
prompt and a positional prompt. These prompts are integrated into the NCA architecture to provide
additional contextual information during the segmentation process. The task prompt specifies the
segmentation target, while the positional prompt defines a starting point, allowing the model to better
localize anatomical regions.
To integrate the prompts into the NCA architecture, the task prompt is concatenated directly to the
state of each cell in the NCA grid (Figure 2 A), ensuring task-specific context is available throughout
the update process. Additionally, the task prompt is processed by two small networks consisting of
1 × 1 convolutions and a SiLU activation, with its output applied multiplicatively to two intermediate
feature maps during each NCA update (Figure 2 B). For simplicity, Figure 2 shows the architecture
for one level, but the same process is applied to both the coarse and fine-resolution models m1 and
m2. Assuming a meaningful task representation is stored in the task space, the model can dynamically
adapt to different segmentation tasks by simply modifying the task vector prompt, without the need
for retraining.
3.3
Genetic Task Vector
We propose a novel formulation where each input image x ∈RH×W ×C is paired with a latent task
vector t ∈Rd, which specifies the segmentation target. The task vector t allows the model to adapt to
different segmentation tasks. Unlike traditional multi-class segmentation models, we do not provide
explicit task labels or predefined class embeddings.
To optimize t for a given [image x, mask y] pair, we use genetic optimization (GO), which iteratively
refines a task vector Pool Pt(x) based on segmentation performance (Algorithm 1). This process
converges to a region in task space that yields high segmentation performance for the target anatomy,
without requiring the model to be explicitly told what the anatomy is.
This optimization enables the NCA to decouple the concepts of what to segment and how to segment,
facilitating foundation-like generalization within a compact architecture. Once an optimal task vector
t∗is identified for a class through genetic optimization, it can be reused for other images of the same
class at inference time. Importantly, we discover task vectors for unseen tasks by optimizing t
using only a single annotated scan from a held-out validation patient, enabling model reuse with
minimal supervision and efficient segmentation across unseen tasks.

3.5
Two-stage training
To improve training stability and prompt utilization, we adopt a two-stage training strategy. We
observed that when both the task prompt t and the positional prompt p are introduced from the start,
the model tends to ignore t, as it is initialized to zero and only gradually refined. This limits the
amount of information that can be encoded in the task vector. Conversely, if p is added later, it is
similarly neglected, since the model has already converged without relying on positional information.
Instead, we first train the model using only t, allowing it to optimize a meaningful task embedding
space via standard supervision L(fθ(x, t), y) (see Algorithm 2). After this phase, we reset the model
weights θ, retain the learned task prompts t, and begin training with both t and p, this time keeping t
fixed, hence repeating Algorithm 2 without the genetic optimization. This ensures the model learns
to effectively leverage both prompts from the start, leading to more consistent convergence and better
overall performance.
4
Experimental Setup and Results
We evaluate Foundational-NCA on segmentation accuracy, generalization to unseen tasks, and the
structure of its learned task space. Implemented in PyTorch [22], our experiments benchmark
performance against state-of-the-art foundation models while highlighting the benefits of prompt-
based control in a compact architecture.
5


4.1
Datasets
We evaluate our method on the TotalSegmentator dataset [31], which provides 1,228 CT scans with
annotations for 117 anatomical structures. For training, we select 15 medium- to large-sized targets,
including the gluteus maximus and medius (left and right), hips, kidneys, liver, sacrum, the right
autochthon muscle, and lobes of the right and left lung. To assess generalization, we evaluate on six
anatomies not seen during training: the left autochthon and iliopsoas muscles, left lower lung lobe,
sternum, stomach, and urinary bladder. We exclude low-quality scans where masks extend beyond
the field of view or which show severe scaling artifacts, and randomly select 100 patients for testing,
5 for validation, and 300 slices per task of the remaining patients for training (in total 4500 slices).
All 3D scans are processed as 2D axial slices resized to 164 × 164, with evaluation performed at the
patient level across all slices. Slices lacking positional prompts are treated as empty predictions.

4.2
Quantitative Results
Table 1 reports the Dice scores of Foundational-NCA compared to several foundation models,
including SAM (Version B), SAM 2 (Version 2.1-T), and MedSAM, across both Task Set A (seen
classes) and Task Set B (unseen classes). On Task Set A, Foundational-NCA achieves the highest
average performance (0.845 Dice), outperforming all three foundation models, MedSAM (0.758 Dice)
and SAM (0.325 Dice) and SAM 2 [25] (0.277) by a substantial margin. Despite being two to three
orders of magnitude smaller in parameter count (211k vs. 93M), this demonstrates that task-specific
supervision, even in compact architectures, can exceed the performance of large, general-purpose
foundation models when applied to domain-specific data.
6


On Task Set B, which includes anatomies not used during training, Foundational-NCA generalizes
reasonably well (average Dice: 0.604), substantially outperforming SAM (0.181 Dice) and SAM 2
(0.158 Dice), but trailing MedSAM (0.734 Dice), which benefits from training on over 11 million
images compared to just 4,500 slices for Foundational-NCA. For each unseen class, Foundational-
NCA is evaluated five times using a different randomly selected validation patient to optimize
the task prompt (takes 26.1 Minutes), ensuring a robust estimate of generalization. Given the
parameter disparity, these results illustrate the efficiency of our architecture in achieving competitive
generalization at a fraction of the parameter requirements. In particular, performance is strongest on
structurally similar or overlapping anatomies (e.g., lungs, autochthon), but weaker on highly variable
or deformable targets like the stomach or bladder, suggesting the limits of task adaptation when the
latent space has not been exposed to sufficient anatomical diversity during training.
Looking ahead, we anticipate that increasing task diversity by leveraging larger datasets with hundreds
of anatomical structures will enrich the latent task representation. This would improve generalization,
enabling the model to adapt more accurately to unseen tasks and approach full foundation-model
performance with a fraction of the parameter budget.

4.3
Qualitative Results
The qualitative results in Figure 3 demonstrate Foundational-NCA’s strong performance on seen
segmentation tasks. For tasks such as hippocampus left, Foundational-NCA accurately captures
the target shape, and for the hip, it follows bone contours precisely. Despite being trained on a
smaller dataset of 4,500 images compared to Med-SAM’s 11 million, Foundational-NCA still shows
strong adaptability to unseen tasks. While it struggles with finer details, it effectively fills the target
regions, and the general shape of the anatomical structures is well-preserved. This indicates that
Foundational-NCA can successfully adapt to new tasks and generalize to previously unseen data.
With more data and further training, Foundational-NCA has the potential to close the gap in detail
and achieve similar performance across a wider range of tasks.
4.4
Ablation
To assess the role of each component in Foundational-NCA, we perform an ablation study on the
unseen task set (Table 2), evaluating variants without the task vector ("No Task") and without
the positional prompt ("No Location"). Results for seen tasks are in the supplementary material.
Removing the positional prompt causes a steep performance drop on unseen tasks (average Dice:
0.070), indicating its essential role in spatial localization. Removing the task vector yields moderate
degradation (Dice: 0.551), with especially large losses on anatomically variable structures like
the stomach and bladder. Together, these results show that the positional prompt guides where to
segment, while the task vector helps determine what to segment. Both are critical for enabling
Foundational-NCA to generalize across tasks within such a compact architecture.

4.5
Task Space Organisation
Figure 4 shows how the task space of Foundational-NCA becomes organized through training,
despite the model never receiving supervision about task identities. Instead, genetic optimization
iteratively adjusts each task vector to maximize segmentation accuracy relative to the ground truth.
For visualization, each learned task vector is colored according to the target anatomy it ultimately
segments, although this label is never used during training. The result reveals distinct clustering in
task space: anatomies with similar shape or location tend to occupy nearby regions. This emergent
structure highlights the model’s ability to disentangle and organize tasks purely through the interplay
of gradient descent and genetic optimization, even in the absence of explicit class information.

4.6
Task Vector Interpolation
To illustrate how the task vector influences segmentation, we interpolate between task vectors while
keeping the input image and positional prompt fixed (Figure 5). Interpolating from liver →kidney,
we observe the model initially producing no segmentation in non-liver regions. As the task vector
moves toward the kidney representation, the model gradually segments kidney-like structures aligned
to the spatial context. Further interpolation from kidney →lung lower lobe shows intermediate
states where the entire lung region is briefly highlighted before refining specifically to the lower lobe.
To validate these dynamics, we sample task vectors uniformly from the unit sphere and visualize
predictions on a fixed input image (Figure 6). Each sampled vector is colored according to its
nearest training class mean in task space, demonstrating distinct segmentation behaviors across
different regions. These transitions confirm that task vectors continuously guide predictions, encoding
high-level shape and location priors that modulate segmentation dynamics.
4.7
Limitations
While Foundational-NCA achieves strong performance on seen tasks, its generalization to unseen
targets remains below that of much larger specialized medical foundation models, partly due to the
limited training data, just 4,500 slices across 15 tasks, compared to 11 million images used for training
SAM. Training is also compute-intensive, requiring 93 hours in total (on an RTX 4090 GPU and
AMD Ryzen 5700G CPU). A significant portion of this time stems from genetic optimization, with
each round taking approximately 195 minutes. Currently, genetic and gradient-based optimization
are performed in separate passes. In future work, unifying these passes could reduce training time
and improve generalization, particularly when scaling to larger datasets.
5
Conclusion
This study demonstrates that foundation-model-like generalization can be achieved within the highly
efficient framework of Neural Cellular Automata, paving the way for adaptive yet radically compact
medical AI systems. By integrating gradient-based training with a novel genetic optimization strategy
to evolve latent task representations, we show that multi-task medical segmentation is feasible using
only 211K parameters, orders of magnitude fewer than traditional foundation models. Evaluated
on 15 trained and 6 unseen segmentation tasks, Foundational-NCA achieves an average Dice of
84.5% on seen and 60.4% on unseen targets, demonstrating meaningful task adaptation without
retraining model weights. These results highlight a new class of compact, prompt-driven segmentation
models suitable for deployment in real-world, low-resource healthcare environments. By decoupling
segmentation capabilities from parameter count and computational complexity, Foundational-NCA
lays the groundwork for accessible, reusable, and extensible medical AI. Rather than pursuing
incremental performance with ever-larger models, we propose a shift toward compact, task-adaptive
systems. This points toward a future where medical AI is defined not by infrastructure or cost, but by
efficiency, flexibility, and universal accessibility.

A
Implementation Details
This section outlines key aspects of the genetic optimization (GO) setup and training procedures
used in Foundational-NCA. All models are implemented in PyTorch, and genetic optimization is
performed using a modified version of the pygad library, extended to support multiprocessing.
A.1
Genetic Optimization During Training
Genetic optimization is employed only during the first stage of training, starting from epoch 20 and
continuing through epoch 200.
GA Schedule and Parameters
• Task vector dimension: 3
• Population size: 20
• Initialization: Uniformly sampled from [−1, 1]3, projected onto the unit sphere
• Gene bounds: [−1, 1] (enforced via normalization)
11


• Update interval: Every 8 epochs, linearly increased to every 16
• Generations per GA step: 1 (evaluate, generate offspring, evaluate, select best)
At each genetic step, 19 offspring are generated while the top candidate from the previous generation
is retained. Fitness values are not cached between epochs, as the model evolves and caching would
degrade performance.
Selection, Crossover, and Mutation
• Selection: Tournament selection with k = 3, repeated n = 10 times
• Crossover: Uniform crossover—each gene in the child is selected from one of two parents
at random
• Mutation: Each gene is mutated with probability 0.1; mutation adds a random value from
[−1, 1]
• Normalization: All offspring are projected back onto the unit sphere after mutation
Fitness Function
During training, the inverse of the DiceBCE loss is used as the fitness value.
DiceBCE is also used to train the NCA weights via gradient descent.
A.2
Global Task Vector Optimization
We also optimize global task vectors for each class in two separate settings:
Training Tasks
• 20 epochs of GO
• Population size: 20
• Initialization: Class mean + noise in [−0.1, 0.1], clipped and normalized
• Fitness: Discrete Dice score
Find Task Vector of Unknown Task
• 30 epochs of GO
• Population size: 40
• Initialization: same as for training tasks
• Fitness: Discrete Dice score
A.3
Model Training Setup
We train Foundational-NCA using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 1.6 × 10−4 and β
parameters set to (0.5, 0.5). An exponential learning rate scheduler with a decay rate of γ = 0.9999
is used throughout training.
Training is conducted in two phases: an initial phase of 200 epochs with alternating genetic optimiza-
tion, followed by a second phase of 300 epochs using fixed task vectors. We use a batch size of 30
and a cell fire rate of 0.2, meaning 80% of cells are updated at each NCA step.
The architecture is implemented in PyTorch, and we detail the exact model structure in Figure A.2.
Initialization follows PyTorch defaults, except for two modifications: the input layer (fc0) is
initialized using Xavier initialization, and the skip-connection layer (fc1) is initialized with a
constant value of 0.001.

Training Trajectory
To better understand how Foundational-NCA evolves during training, we examine the optimization
trajectory in more detail.
In Figure 9, we observe several phases of convergence throughout training. Notably, each round of
genetic optimization leads to a distinct improvement in the loss, especially during the early epochs
(e.g., around epoch 20) when the task space is still poorly structured. These early improvements
highlight the importance of cold-start optimization in guiding the model toward more effective task
representations.

Single-Stage Setup:
Beyond removing prompts individually, we investigate the effect of switching
from our proposed two-stage training scheme to a simpler single-stage alternative. In the standard
two-stage setup, we initially train with task prompts only, introducing positional prompts in the
second stage to ensure both types of conditioning are fully utilized. In the single-stage baseline, both
prompts are introduced at epoch 20.
Quantitatively, we observe that this joint conditioning from the start results in notably worse gener-
alization on unseen tasks (Task Set B), with the average Dice score dropping from 0.604 to 0.572.
Although performance on seen tasks (Task Set A) is comparable, the degraded generalization suggests
that the task space becomes less structured when both prompts are introduced simultaneously.
Meaningful Task Separation:
This intuition is confirmed visually in Figure 11, where the task
vectors learned under the single-stage setup show no meaningful separation or organization on the
unit sphere. In contrast, the two-stage strategy encourages the formation of a structured, disentangled
task space, which is critical for generalization.
Further evidence is provided in Figure 12, which compares the UMAP projections of task vectors
under both training strategies. The two-stage setup yields clearly clustered and well-separated task
representations, while the single-stage approach results in overlapping and less distinguishable task
15


regions, supporting our conclusion that careful prompt scheduling is crucial for robust, multi-task
generalization.

Additional Qualitative Results
Task Vector Interpolations:
To further explore the influence of the task prompt on the model’s
predictions, we visualize interpolations from a single starting task vector to four distinct target vectors
in Figure 13. This illustrates how the segmentation output evolves as the task embedding changes.
For example, starting from the vector for Lung Middle Lobe R, we observe distinct and substantial
changes when interpolating toward Autochthon R and Lung Upper Lobe R, highlighting the model’s
sensitivity to semantic shifts in task space. A similar pattern emerges when starting from Gluteus
Medius L, in this case, interpolation toward Hip R even causes the model to segment the anatomically
opposite side, demonstrating how certain transitions in task space can induce asymmetric responses.
Sampling from the Unit Sphere:
We extend the task vector sampling experiment introduced
in the main manuscript. In Figure 15, we sample task vectors uniformly from the unit sphere and
visualize their corresponding outputs on two distinct images in the color of the closest average training
anatomy. This larger-scale evaluation reveals a wide range of segmentation behaviors depending on
the location in the task space. Importantly, we find that the positional prompt also plays a key role: in
Sample 1 (left), where the positional prompt anchors the expected location of the anatomy, the model
produces more consistent predictions across task vectors. In contrast, Sample 2 (right), exhibits more
pronounced variability depending on the task vector, emphasizing the interplay between task and
spatial conditioning.
Additional Qualitative Results:
Finally, we provide an extended set of qualitative results in
Figure 15, covering all 15 training tasks and 6 unseen tasks. On the seen tasks, Foundational-
NCA demonstrates robust segmentation performance, often capturing fine anatomical details and
accommodating large structural variation, for instance, the liver’s varying morphology. For unseen
tasks, performance varies depending on how well the new class is represented in the learned task
space. While certain classes like Hip R are segmented with high fidelity, others such as Lung Lower
Lobe L show inconsistencies, especially in more complex regions. These findings underscore the
model’s capacity for generalization, while also highlighting the challenges posed by underrepresented
or highly variable anatomies. This limitation is likely exacerbated by the relatively small training
